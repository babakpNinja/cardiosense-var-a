Every forty seconds, someone in the United States with heart failure begins retaining the fluid that will eventually send them to a hospital. Cardiosense changed that, and a first-of-its-kind technology reached clinicians safely because of regulatory science as much as sensors and algorithms.

On May 27, 2026, the FDA granted De Novo classification to Cardiosense's PCWP Analysis Software™, the first noninvasive, AI-powered tool ever authorized to estimate pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, a key indicator of heart health that until now required an invasive procedure.

The ProblemSix million patients, and a pressure no one could see

Heart failure affects roughly 6 million American adults and is the single most expensive condition for the U.S. healthcare system. The driver is congestion, whose earliest signal is pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), the pressure in the left heart. But it could be measured only invasively, so for most patients it stayed invisible.

The ScienceTwo decades of signal in one sensor

Cardiosense grew out of nearly twenty years of research into seismocardiography (SCG), which measures each heartbeat's tiny vibrations at the chest. Combined with the electrocardiogram (ECG) and the photoplethysmogram (PPG), those signals encode pressure. The CardioTag™ device captures all three from a single chest patch, and Cardiosense's clinical AI turns the raw waveforms into a number a clinician can act on.

The Key Evidence · SEISMIC-HF I

Clinical evidence, measured against the invasive gold standard

SEISMIC-HF I set a high bar for clinical evidence: a prospective, multi-center study that developed and validated the algorithm in 310 patients with HFrEF, each measured head-to-head against right-heart catheterization, the invasive gold standard for PCWP. Published in JACC: Heart Failure and presented at AHA 2024, it matched the accuracy of implanted pressure sensors, evidence strong enough to carry a first-in-class device through FDA review.

From Algorithm to Authorized DeviceHow a model becomes a medicine

Regulatory strategy meets clinical study design here: SEISMIC-HF I compared the algorithm against simultaneous invasive PCWP with acceptance criteria set in advance, enrolled a population broad enough to generalize, and kept training data apart from test data.

The authorized Indications for Use bind the company and the clinician to what the FDA reviewed: the software estimates PCWP and flags patients above or below 18 mmHg in adults with HFrEF and NYHA Class II to IV symptoms, for adjunctive use alongside standard care, under a quality system compliant with 21 CFR Part 820 and ISO 13485.

"A clinical algorithm doesn't become trustworthy the moment it's accurate. It becomes trustworthy when you can show, with pre-specified evidence, in the right patients, under a quality system, that it will keep being accurate." Arezou Azar, PhD · Chief Regulatory & Compliance Officer, Cardiosense

The FDA PartnershipBuilding a new device type with CDRH

Because no noninvasive PCWP device had ever existed, there was no predicate to point to, and a device without a predicate cannot take the usual 510(k) route. It needs the pathway built for genuinely novel, low-to-moderate-risk technology: the De Novo classification request. De Novo does not merely authorize a product; it asks the FDA to create a new device classification and to write the special controls every future device of this type must meet.

Cardiosense worked with the cardiovascular reviewers in the FDA's Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) through the pre-submission process, aligning on the reference standard and special controls before the formal submission. The software also holds FDA Breakthrough Device Designation, reserved for technologies that treat life-threatening conditions with no cleared alternative.

The Future, Pre-AuthorizedPCCP: how the algorithm keeps getting better, safely

A traditional device is frozen at authorization: any meaningful change to the algorithm has historically required a new submission. But an AI model keeps improving over time.

The Predetermined Change Control Plan (PCCP) removes that choice. Authorized by the FDA Omnibus Reform Act of 2022 and detailed in the FDA's December 2024 guidance, it lets a manufacturer specify in advance which modifications an AI model may undergo, along with the methods that will validate them, and then have the FDA authorize that plan up front. As long as an update stays inside the pre-authorized envelope, the improved model can reach clinicians without a new submission each time.

The De Novo grant (DEN250057) expressly authorized a PCCP for the PCWP Analysis Software, with a postmarket requirement to monitor real-world performance across the intended population.

"A Predetermined Change Control Plan is, to me, the most patient-centered idea in modern device regulation. It lets us promise a clinician something we never could before: the algorithm you rely on will keep improving, and every improvement will have already cleared a bar the FDA agreed to. That's how you make sure the best version of the technology is the one that actually reaches the bedside." Arezou Azar, PhD · Chief Regulatory & Compliance Officer, Cardiosense

The PayoffWhat this changes for doctors and patients

A clinician can now read a patient's key heart-filling pressure from a sensor on the chest instead of a catheter or implant. That supports pressure-guided care and lets physicians titrate medication to a real number rather than a delayed symptom, keeping patients out of the hospital.

A breakthrough only reaches a patient when the regulatory function designs the proof and earns the FDA's confidence that the technology is safe and reproducibly effective. With that change-control strategy in place, the authorization is a starting point, not a finish line.
